Frequency filtering

Layers and the spectrum

Last lab, a kernel slid over the pixels to blur or to find edges. This lab does the same jobs a second way: through frequencies.

A frequency is how many times a pattern repeats. Smooth areas change slowly: low frequency. Edges, fine texture and noise change fast: high frequency.

A row of pixels is a sum of layers. A layer is a simple pattern that repeats. Add the layers pixel by pixel and you get the row:

Delete the fast layer and the row becomes 150 100 50 100: smoother. That is a blur. An image works the same way: every layer covers the whole image, and each pixel adds one piece from each layer.

To find out how much of a layer is in the image: multiply the image by the layer and add everything. A big total: the layer is there. A total of 0: it is not. Do it for every possible layer and you get a grid of numbers, the spectrum:

This multiply-and-add for every layer is the Fourier transform (np.fft.fft2 or cv2.dft). The inverse transform adds the layers back: nothing is lost.

Demo: your image and its spectrum

What to look for: pick the sign images in the image bar. The middle of the spectrum holds the slow layers; the farther from the middle, the faster the layer. The direction from the middle is the direction of the change. Bright means a lot of that layer.

A filter is a mask

To filter, multiply the spectrum by a mask: 1 keeps a layer, 0 deletes it. Then go back with the inverse transform. The five steps are always the same; only the mask changes from filter to filter.

Good to know: kernel or frequency? Sliding a kernel in the image is the same as multiplying by a mask in the spectrum (the lecture: convolution in space = multiplication in frequency). A small kernel is cheaper; a big filter, or a pattern that repeats, is easier in frequency.

Noise is a fast change from one pixel to the next, so it lives far from the middle of the spectrum. Keep the middle and delete the rest: the noise goes. This is a lowpass filter, a blur. The cutoff D0 is how far from the middle you still keep.

The mask can stop sharply or softly. Ideal: 1 inside a circle, 0 outside (sharp cut, it leaves ripples near edges). Gaussian: a soft bell. Butterworth: in between; its order n sets how sharp the step is.

Ideal: 1 if D ≤ D0, else 0 · Gaussian: e−D²/2D0² · Butterworth: 1 / (1 + (D/D0)2n), D = distance to the middle.

Demo: lowpass

What to look for: choose sign_noisy.png in the image bar. Make D0 small: the noise goes, then the letters blur too. Compare the ideal mask (ripples) with the Gaussian.

Blur, two ways. A Gaussian mask in the spectrum is a Gaussian kernel in the image (the lecture: a Gaussian stays a Gaussian). On sign_noisy.png, scored against the clean sign (average difference, lower is better): noisy 31.2 · cv2.GaussianBlur(img, (0, 0), 1.5) 9.4 · Gaussian mask, D0 = 30: 9.3.

A lowpass mask keeps…

Slow layers sit near the middle; keeping only them removes the fast changes: noise, and also some detail.

Now do the opposite: delete the middle and keep the outside. Only the fast changes stay: the edges. This is a highpass filter. Its mask is simply 1 minus the lowpass mask.

The highpass is what the blur took away. Add it back to the image and the edges get stronger: sharpening, like unsharp masking in the last lab.

highpass = 1 − lowpass · edges = |result| · sharpened = image + result.

Demo: highpass

What to look for: choose sign.png. With a small D0 the edges are thick; with a big D0 only the finest details stay.

The highpass mask is…

Where the lowpass keeps (1), the highpass deletes (0), and the other way round.

Stripes are one layer that repeats many times, all in one direction. In the spectrum they are just two bright dots. A blur would blur everything. A mask that is 0 only on the two dots removes just the stripes: a notch filter.

Where are the dots? The stripes on sign_stripes.png repeat every 8 pixels, so 320 / 8 = 40 times across the image: the dots are 40 cells left and right of the middle.

mask = 1 everywhere, 0 in a small circle on each dot: cv2.circle(mask, (w // 2 ± 40, h // 2), 3, 0, -1).
