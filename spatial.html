Spatial filtering

You are a pixel and you want a new value. The only way to get one is to ask your neighbours what they think. But not every opinion counts the same. The kernel is the list that says how much you listen to each neighbour. Its numbers are the coefficients.

Three recipes on the neighbours 10 10 200 (you are the middle one):

One line of math: new value = sum of (neighbour × coefficient). An image kernel does it on a 3 × 3 window:

Good to know: correlation and convolution. Sliding the kernel as it is and adding the products is correlation. Convolution is the same with the kernel turned 180° (read the list back to front). For symmetric kernels (box, Gaussian, Laplacian) both give the same result; for Sobel the sign flips. cv2.filter2D computes correlation, and so does everything on this site.
Practical note, not from the lecture: template matching and the "convolution" layers of neural networks are correlation too.

import cv2
import numpy as np

gray = cv2.cvtColor(cv2.imread("landscape.png"), cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
same = np.array([[0, 0, 0], [0, 1, 0], [0, 0, 0]], np.float32)   # listen only to yourself
blur = np.ones((3, 3), np.float32) / 9                           # everyone counts 1/9
print(np.array_equal(cv2.filter2D(gray, -1, same), gray))                   # True
print(np.array_equal(cv2.filter2D(gray, -1, blur), cv2.blur(gray, (3, 3))))  # True

Every coefficient of a 3 × 3 kernel is 1/9. The filter…

Each pixel becomes the average of its 9 neighbours, so details are smoothed out.

You are a pixel on the edge of the image. Some of your neighbours do not exist, but the kernel still needs 9 values. So you invent them: this is padding. Four ways, on the row 10 20 30 40 50 with two missing neighbours on the left:

It matters. A 3-wide box on a row of 100s: the edge pixel gets (0 + 100 + 100) / 3 ≈ 67 with zeros, and 100 with any of the other three.

In OpenCV: borderType=cv2.BORDER_CONSTANT for zeros. The default is BORDER_REFLECT_101 (practical note, not from the lecture).

A box filter listens to every neighbour the same. A Gaussian filter listens more to the close ones, and less and less to the far ones. On 10 10 10 200 200 (you are the middle one): box, 1/5 each, gives 86. Gaussian-like weights 1 4 6 4 1 / 16 give 69: you stay closer to yourself.

With a box you choose the size. With a Gaussian you choose σ: how far you still listen. For the same blur a Gaussian needs a bigger kernel (lecture: box 71 × 71 vs Gaussian 151 × 151, σ = 25).

Gaussian kernel size ≈ 6σ + 1. Both filters are separable: one pass along the rows, one along the columns.

To find an edge, ask your neighbours: "is the right side different from the left side?" The recipe -1 0 1 (right minus left) gives 0 on a flat area and a big number on an edge:

Sobel does the same on three rows at once, and the middle row counts double. One kernel finds vertical edges, another finds horizontal ones. To know how strong an edge is whatever its direction, combine them like a walk: 3 m east and 4 m north is 5 m away. That is the magnitude of the gradient. Do not just add them: the signs can cancel.

dx (vertical edges) = [-1 0 1; -2 0 2; -1 0 1], dy (horizontal edges) = [-1 -2 -1; 0 0 0; 1 2 1], strength = √(dx² + dy²). The lecture slide calls them gx / gy the other way round (it takes x as the row direction); we use OpenCV's names.

On a flat area, a Sobel kernel gives…

Its coefficients add up to 0, so equal neighbours cancel out.

Every pixel lives in a little box in memory, and the size of the box decides what fits in it (the data type):

  • CV_8U (uint8): whole numbers 0…255, 1 byte. Anything outside is cut: −190 → 0, 760 → 255 (saturation).
  • CV_32F: decimals and negative numbers, 4 bytes.
  • CV_64F: even more precision, 8 bytes, rarely needed.

An 800 × 600 gray image takes 0.48 MB, 1.92 MB or 3.84 MB. Sobel and Laplacian give negative numbers, so with CV_8U about half of the edges silently disappear.

Safe recipe: compute in float, take the absolute value or the magnitude, then cut to 0…255 and go back to uint8 to show it.

Sharpening makes an image look clearer: find the edges and push them apart, so the dark side of each edge gets darker and the bright side brighter.

The Laplacian asks: "am I different from my 4 neighbours?" It adds the 4 neighbours and takes away 4 × you. On a flat area it gives 0. On a 10 10 200 200 edge it gives +190 on the dark side and −190 on the bright side. Subtract it from the image and the edge gets stronger. With the wrong sign the textures get softer and the edges get odd outlines.

Second way, unsharp masking: blur the image, subtract the blur from the original. The leftover (the "mask") holds the edges and fine details. Add it back, times k. k = 1 is unsharp masking, k > 1 is highboost (the lecture compares k = 1 and 4.5). Too big a k looks like drawn outlines (practical note, not from the lecture).

Laplacian kernel [0 1 0; 1 -4 1; 0 1 0]; sharpened = original − Laplacian (lecture: f + c·Laplacian, c = −1); unsharp: g = f + k·(f − blur).

import cv2
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

gray = cv2.cvtColor(cv2.imread("landscape.png"), cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
lap = cv2.Laplacian(gray, cv2.CV_32F)
sharp = np.clip(gray - lap, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)   # minus: the centre of the kernel is -4

blur = cv2.GaussianBlur(gray, (0, 0), 5)
mask = gray.astype(np.float32) - blur
k1 = np.clip(gray + 1 * mask, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)     # unsharp masking
k45 = np.clip(gray + 4.5 * mask, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)  # highboost

for i, (name, img) in enumerate([("gray", gray), ("gray - lap", sharp), ("k = 1", k1), ("k = 4.5", k45)]):
    plt.subplot(1, 4, i + 1)
    plt.imshow(img, cmap="gray", vmin=0, vmax=255)
    plt.title(name)
    plt.axis("off")
plt.show()
